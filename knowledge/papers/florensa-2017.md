# Reverse Curriculum Generation for Reinforcement Learning (Florensa, Held, Wulfmeier, Zhang & Abbeel, CoRL 2017)

**Lab:** UC Berkeley (Abbeel group) · **arXiv:** 1707.05300 · **Venue:** CoRL 2017 (1st Conference on Robot Learning) · **Read state:** abstract depth + BAIR blog post + proceedings page (full PDF not fetched; SoID mechanism and algorithm described from secondary sources; complexity argument inferred from mechanism, not formally extracted from the paper's theorems)

## What it is

Florensa et al. propose training a goal-conditioned policy by **starting the agent near the goal and progressively shifting the start-state distribution backward** as the agent masters easier starts. The algorithm requires only one prior: a single achieved goal state. From that state, it generates a Brownian-motion random walk to sample nearby starts, trains the policy, evaluates success rates, filters to "Starts of Intermediate Difficulty" (SoID: start states where the policy succeeds between R_min and R_max, empirically 10–90%), and repeats — each round sampling new starts from the current SoID frontier, which has moved further from the goal. The result is an automatically adapting curriculum that solves previously intractable sparse-reward manipulation and navigation tasks without manual reward shaping or expert demonstrations.

## Why it matters here

Florensa 2017 is the **theoretical anchor for our exp0066 backward-imagination curriculum**. The core translation:

| Florensa 2017 (real environment) | Our exp0066 (RSSM imagination) |
|---|---|
| Start-state distribution begins near the goal | Imagination seeds begin at buffered prefix-complete latents (step k-1 done) |
| Policy trains on short-horizon episodes from those starts | Actor-critic trains on imagined rollouts from those frontier latents |
| SoID filter: advance frontier when success rate 10–90% | Analogous: advance to step k+1 once step k success rate is stable |
| Brownian walk generates nearby starts | Replay buffer accumulates nearby real-trajectory latents organically |
| Gradually walks backward toward episode start | Gradually shifts seeding from step k-1 latents toward step k-2, etc. |

**The exploration complexity argument** (qualitative, since full paper was not read at theorem depth): Florensa motivates the curriculum by noting that a random-exploration policy starting from the episode origin reaches a goal state with probability that **decreases rapidly with goal distance** — the product of small per-step action-success probabilities. Reverse curriculum eliminates this decay by always starting the agent close enough to the goal that the current policy can reach it with reasonable probability, then incrementally expanding the start set. The exp0066 version of this argument is our p^(k-1) visitation decay: the probability of reaching step k starting from step 0 is roughly the product of k single-step success probabilities, which for our length-2 gestures with step success ~28% gives p² ≈ 8% — exactly the "exactly-correct" floor we observe. Seeding from prefix-complete latents removes the product and replaces it with a single-step problem.

**Key mechanism mapping.** Florensa uses the **real environment** to reset to nearby start states, which is only possible in simulation. Our imagination-in-RSSM is the enabling technology that makes this work for our setting: we cannot reset the real environment to a "step-k-1-complete" state, but we can **seed imagination** from a buffered latent that is a real observed state at that condition. This is the design insight the QUEUE entry already articulates; Florensa validates the principle, imagination is what makes it feasible for us.

## Details

**SoID definition.** A start state s₀ is a "Start of Intermediate Difficulty" for policy π if R_min < E[R(π, s₀)] < R_max. States outside this band — too easy (success ≈ 1) or too hard (success ≈ 0) — produce zero gradient signal and are filtered from the training distribution. The curriculum's key property is that useful starts always exist in the SoID band: as the policy improves, states that were too hard become intermediate, and the frontier naturally advances backward from the goal.

**State generation mechanism.** New candidate starts are generated by **taking random exploratory actions from existing SoID states** and recording visited states. This Brownian-motion walk in state space finds nearby states without requiring a distance metric. The step size (how many random actions) controls the rate of curriculum advance. A replay buffer of all past SoID states prevents the policy from forgetting how to reach the goal from already-mastered regions.

**Algorithm (abstract-level reconstruction):**
1. Seed with s₀ = s_goal.
2. Generate N candidate starts by Brownian walk from current SoID set.
3. Evaluate policy success rate on each candidate.
4. Keep only SoID candidates (10–90% success). Add to replay buffer.
5. Train policy on mixture of new SoID starts + replay buffer.
6. Repeat.

**Results.** Solves point-mass maze, ant maze (sparse binary reward, no distance shaping), and robotic manipulation tasks (pushing, repositioning) that standard RL with dense reward shaping cannot solve. Demonstrates transfer to a wide range of start positions once the curriculum reaches the episode origin.

**Scope note.** This paper operates entirely in simulation with real environment resets — it does not use a learned dynamics model and has no discussion of world models, imagination, or RSSM-style training. The `scope: shared` designation reflects that the reverse curriculum principle is a general RL insight applicable beyond our project.

## Open questions

- Does the Florensa paper include a formal theorem bounding how the curriculum's complexity scales with goal distance (e.g., O(k) training episodes vs O(exp(k)) for random exploration)? Not confirmed at abstract read depth; the BAIR blog post and proceedings page give only qualitative arguments.
- The SoID filter (10–90%) is empirical. Is there a theoretical justification for these thresholds, or should our analog (when to advance the frontier from step k to step k-1) use a different criterion?
- In our setting, the "Brownian walk in state space" is replaced by the replay buffer accumulating real prefix-complete latents organically. If prefix-complete examples are too sparse (e.g., step k-2-complete latents never appear because the curriculum hasn't reached there yet), we may need a more active seeding mechanism — closer to Florensa's explicit walk.
